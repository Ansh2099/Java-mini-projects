ATTACK 1 [MAJOR] The main Business Central pitch rests on a gap Microsoft's own docs say is already covered, and misses the real limits
Where: Short answer 3 and Part 2 competitor table, "Business Central firms whose orders break Microsoft's agent (customer part codes, Excel, Gmail, mixed languages)"; Part 7 pick 1 and Order of work, "contact 30 Business Central distributors with messy orders".
Problem: The solution cites the Sales Order Agent FAQ (source 2) as read in full. That same page lists "item references" among the agent's tested quote scenarios. Item references are the Business Central feature that maps a customer's own part numbers to your item numbers. So "customer part codes" is the gap this solution leans on most, and it is not a proven gap. "Gmail" is also weak. A firm that runs Business Central almost always runs Microsoft 365 and Exchange, and the solution gives no evidence that Business Central firms on Gmail exist in numbers. The FAQ does list limits that would make a real pitch: no more than 15 item lines per document, variant codes left empty, only sales lines of type "Item", no changes to orders (only to quotes), and customer lookup only by an exact email match (which also breaks on forwarded emails). The solution uses only the email match. So the direct sales channel (30 Business Central prospects from week 4) opens with a pitch Microsoft's docs already answer. Its strongest angles go unused.

ATTACK 2 [MAJOR] The top niche has no edge the solution can show against the competitors it names
Where: Short answer 3, "Best niche: UK distributors on Sage 200"; Part 2, "Against Zestcode your only real edge is price (your costs are lower)"; Part 4, Hyperfox check, "It is cheapest for slow, messy orders with few lines, which is exactly the messy niche you want."
Problem: The case for the niche is "no prebuilt Sage 200 connector at Hyperfox". But the solution also finds that Zestcode, a UK agency, already sells this exact service on Sage 200, with a working connector and a person in the UK. Against Zestcode it says the only edge is price. Yet it lists Zestcode's price as "Not public", so the claim has nothing behind it. Against Hyperfox, its own table shows Hyperfox is cheaper in the messy niche: EUR 145 to 220 a month at 4 hours of keying and 8 to 12 minutes per order, against this solution's upkeep of 270 to 405 USD. Then add the fact that the team cannot use the Sage 200 API without a £3,125 a year programme. The number one pick is a niche where a local rival already delivers, a product rival charges less, and the team cannot build without a middleman. The user asked for "the objectively best advice". The solution states all of these facts but never checks them against its own ranking.

ATTACK 3 [MAJOR] The "no fee that grows with volume" selling point contradicts the solution's own pricing formula
Where: Part 4, "You win on no 12 month lock in ..., no setup fee surprise, a build that includes their odd rules, and no fee that grows with volume."
Problem: The solution's own pricing formula (Assumptions 7) sets upkeep at a third to a half of the monthly saving, and the saving is measured hours of keying multiplied by salary. More orders means more keying hours, which means higher upkeep. Its own tables show it: upkeep goes from 270 to 405 USD at 4 hours up to 1,625 to 2,440 USD at 24 hours. That is a fee that grows with volume, the same thing it criticises Hyperfox for. It is also one of the four reasons the solution gives for winning a deal where it has already said Hyperfox is cheaper. If a buyer compares the two quotes, this reason falls apart.

ATTACK 4 [MAJOR] The "independent" hedge is not independent, and its numbers are unsourced
Where: Short answer 5, "Your independent second European workflow is customer remittances into cash allocation"; Part 7 pick 4, "as the independent hedge"; against Part 2, "What it does not hedge: it still needs Sage 200 access"; and the fallback ladder step 2, "Fails if Sage 200 access stays closed".
Problem: Step 1 of the ladder (order intake on Sage 200) and step 2 (cash allocation) share the same blocker: getting into Sage 200. Xero, QuickBooks and Business Central are ruled out for cash allocation. So if the partner path fails, both steps fail together. This breaks the ladder's own rule that "each step fails for a different reason than the one above it." On top of that, the task requires "d. How often it happens and how long it takes." For this workflow the row says "Unsourced" and gives only an estimate. The RFQ workflow says "Unsourced" too. The one workflow sold as the hedge has no sourced frequency or time data. Its evidence for Sage 200 demand is rated "low" by the solution itself.

ATTACK 5 [MINOR] The price bands do not match the solution's own value table
Where: Part 4, "What to charge", Small EU/UK, "400 to 600 USD at 6 to 8 hours"; also Part 2 row g.
Problem: The value table two sections above gives upkeep of 405 to 610 USD at 6 hours and 540 to 815 USD at 8 hours. So the 6 to 8 hour band should be about 405 to 815 USD, not 400 to 600. A small firm with 8 measured hours would be under quoted by up to about 215 USD a month against the solution's own formula. The formula says quote from measured hours, so the user cannot tell which of the two numbers to use.
