ATTACK 1 [FATAL] The "gap" behind the top pick is not real: it misses ERP-agnostic statement tools, and its own check is built so it cannot find them
Where: E1 f, "The gap, stated honestly: a medium Dutch firm on Exact Online can buy an enterprise AP platform or a manual Excel matcher, and nothing in between that I could find." Also the 4 step check, step 4: "Search '<ledger name> supplier statement reconciliation' ... If a product under about 300 USD a month shows up, stop."
Problem: Pick 1 depends completely on Exact Online being uncovered. But mid-market tools that sell supplier statement reconciliation against any ERP exist, and none of them are in the solution. 360° AP's "AP Accurate" says it imports supplier statements "in any format" (PDF, Excel, CSV) and reconciles them against "your ERP", offers a free proof of concept, and its pricing page is titled "Plans From $100/mo" (https://360-ap.com/ap-accurate, https://360-ap.com/pricing). Open ECX reads supplier PDFs, matches them against the purchase ledger and flags only exceptions (https://www.openecx.com/statement-reconciliation). FlexTecs FlexTrap takes statements "in any format" and matches them "across every connected ERP" (https://flextecs.com/statement-reconciliation). Telic Digital sells the same thing (https://telic.digital/automated-supplier-statement-reconciliation/). Each of these fills the space between "enterprise platform" and "Excel matcher." So the claim "nothing in between" is false. The check also breaks. Step 4 searches with the ledger's name, so a tool that says "any ERP" and never names Exact will not show up. A user who runs the check on Exact exactly as written gets "empty" and pitches, and then the finance manager finds 360° AP in one search. My confidence: high that these tools exist and cover Exact through file upload or a generic connector. Medium that AP Accurate's own tier falls under the solution's 300 USD cutoff, because the page shows a starting plan price, not a price for this module. Even at medium, that is enough to flip the brief's test ("If it is already solved by a built in feature or a cheap tool, reject it") for the only European pick.

ATTACK 3 [MAJOR] The "100 statements a month" qualifier comes from wrong math
Where: E1 "Below 100 statements a month, the fee is higher than the full job's labour saving and you lose the renewal. Walk away." Step 5: "At that size, full coverage is worth about £800 or €1,250 of labour a month ... Below it, the fee beats the saving and they cancel."
Problem: The solution's own numbers put one statement at 0.5 h. That is €12.50 in NL (at €25/h) or £8 in the UK (at £16/h). The €250 to €450 fee equals full-coverage labour at 20 to 36 statements in NL. In the UK it is about 28 to 50 statements (€250 to €450 is about £220 to £396 at the stated exchange rates). So the fee is not "higher than the full job's labour saving" below 100. For example, 60 statements a month in NL means €750 of full-coverage labour, well above a €450 fee. The rule would still turn that firm away. Going the other way, the solution itself says firms really reconcile only their top 20 today, about €250 of spend. So at 100 statements the fee (€250 to €450) is equal to or higher than what the firm spends now. Labour at 100 statements does not predict renewal either way. Renewal rests on "money found," and the only source for that is Fiscal's own marketing. The document calls this "the qualifier everywhere," yet it is not derived from the numbers it shows.

ATTACK 4 [MAJOR] The India top pick costs more than the labour it replaces, by the solution's own numbers
Where: I1 d "Big parties quarterly, all parties at year end ... 20 to 60 minutes per party"; I1 e "₹15,000 to ₹45,000 a month"; I1 g "₹20,000 to ₹40,000 setup, ₹3,000 to ₹6,000 a month"; Step 8 pilot "Reconcile its top 30 parties".
Problem: The brief says an Indian workflow "only makes sense where automation clearly beats simply hiring another person, or where speed and errors matter more than salary." Take the solution's own pilot scope: 30 parties a quarter at 20 to 60 minutes is 10 to 30 hours a quarter, or 3.3 to 10 hours a month. At ₹15,000 to ₹45,000 for about 200 working hours, that is ₹75 to ₹225 an hour, so ₹250 to ₹2,250 of labour a month. The fee is ₹3,000 to ₹6,000 a month plus up to ₹40,000 setup. Only an outlier matches the fee: 100 parties every quarter at 60 minutes, paid at the top salary, which gives about ₹7,200 a month. The solution never names an error cost or speed cost that would close this gap for a trader. So on its own figures, I1 fails the India test for the typical client it plans to pilot with.

ATTACK 6 [MAJOR] The 90-day plan builds first and tests the pick-killing risk last
Where: Step 8, items 1 to 5. The build runs "1 to 14 October," India pilots run "15 October to 31 December," and Europe discovery starts only in "Weeks 6 to 13 (mid November to end of December)." The stop rule needs "10 calls with firms that pass the 4 step check and the 100 statement qualifier."
Problem: The solution says the "Biggest open risk" is whether Dutch firms receive 100+ statements a month, and that "Your first 5 Dutch calls must answer" it. Answering that takes a few calls and no code. Yet the plan spends about 6 weeks of part-time build plus two India pilots before the first Dutch call. The 10-call stop rule cannot fire before the end of December, and in practice it lands in January because of Christmas. If Dutch firms turn out not to work on monthly statements, which the solution cannot rule out, the team loses about three months. For a part-time, two-person team with a 50 USD budget, the order should be reversed. As written, the user would follow a plan that puts the cheapest test last.
