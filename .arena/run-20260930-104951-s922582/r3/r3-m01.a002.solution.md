# Workflow automations SMEs will pay for: India and Europe (September 2026)

## The short answer

Build one thing: **customer orders that arrive as messages become draft sales orders in the system where the client actually keys orders, and a person clicks approve.**

* **Europe (your paying market):** sell this as a **setup and upkeep service**, not as a product tied to one app. Start in Finland, where Developer A already has a paying client. The first question to every lead is **"In which system do you type your sales orders?"** That system is the target, not the accounting system.
  * **Best Finnish target found: Rackbeat**, the stock and order system Visma sells as "Netvisor Varasto" to Netvisor users (also used with e-conomic in Denmark). Orders are entered there, it has an open API that Netvisor says is free to use, and I found no email order reader for it.
  * **Second: Procountor or Netvisor on their own**, for smaller firms with no separate stock system that type orders straight into the accounting system's sales order screen. These firms have lower volume, so fewer of them pass the money test.
  * **Walk away from:** Lemonsoft (it now ships its own AI "Tilausapuri" that reads email orders), Microsoft Dynamics 365 Business Central (built in Sales Order Agent), Katana (built in assistant plus an app), Fortnox, Cin7 Core, Unleashed, Exact Online and Odoo (ready tools on each).
* **Finland is contested, not empty.** Finnish firms already sell this exact service: Netum (custom, 2 to 3 months), Hurja Solutions (built it on Odoo), Zimply (a product that claims "Visma" support), and general AI agencies like Kasvuraketti (€4,900 for one automation). You win on a lower fixed price, speed, and item matching that learns each customer's words. That is a thinner edge than "nobody does this". Say it plainly to yourselves.
* **UK: second, not first.** The UK system with the clearest gap, Sage 200, charges developers about £5,000 a year to build against its API, and a service firm (Zestcode) already sells this on it. Add Sage 200 only after revenue, or through a reseller who holds the licence.
* **India (your case study):** the same engine, with WhatsApp orders (typed text, voice notes, photos of lists) becoming sales orders in TallyPrime for one Ludhiana distributor. This is **not an Indian product**: Zotok, Karvan and HublerX already sell it. You build it once, at cost, to get real before and after numbers.

Five hard truths up front:

1. **The order entry market is being squeezed from both sides.** ERP makers build it in (Microsoft, Lemonsoft, Katana), ready tools cover the popular systems, and local consultancies sell custom builds. Your opening is systems nobody has reached yet, plus setup, item matching and custom rules. That window may last one to two years. Keep the ERP connector a small swappable part and re-check competitors before every pitch.
2. **The number that decides every sale is review time, not order volume.** If checking a prepared draft takes 1.5 minutes, 40 orders a day pays well. If it takes 2.5 minutes, you need about 75. So you measure review time in a paid pilot before the monthly fee starts. Part 1 shows the math.
3. **Some ERPs charge you just to build.** Sage 200 failed this check. Rackbeat, Procountor and Netvisor pass it.
4. **Your price guess is roughly right.** A build fee of $1,500 to $4,000 fits small firms; medium firms can pay up to about $6,000. Monthly upkeep of $400 to $1,500 holds, but small firms sit at the bottom ($400 to $600), and only when the measured saving is at least twice the fee.
5. **Your real problem is not the build. It is getting the first European client from Ludhiana with no case study,** against Finnish speaking competitors. The 16 week plan at the end deals with that in about 20 combined hours a week.

## Assumptions

1. **Exchange rates** (rough working rates, check before you quote): £1 ≈ $1.33, €1 ≈ $1.16, $1 ≈ ₹88. All prices exclude VAT or GST.
2. **"A few weeks of part time work"** means about 100 to 120 hours for one workflow. At 20 combined hours a week, that is 5 to 6 calendar weeks per build. The plan assumes **one build at a time**.
3. **"Europe" includes the UK.** Start in Finland (warm contact, free developer access on the target systems, Finnish orders that Microsoft's agent does not officially support), then add the UK once revenue can carry a Sage developer licence.
4. **How I researched.** I used web search and Microsoft Learn. Direct page fetching was blocked for most sites in my setup (including procountor.fi, lemonsoft.fi, rackbeat.com and support.netvisor.fi), so for most links the facts I quote are what the search results showed from that page. The Microsoft Learn pages I read in full. Vendor numbers are vendor claims. Where I have no source, I write **"estimate"** and say how to check it.
5. **Order volume has no public source for any one firm.** So every money figure is shown at several volumes, and you measure the real number in week one.
6. **Time per order** comes from vendor benchmarks for manual entry. **Review time per order has no public source at all.** It is the most important number here, so it gets its own sensitivity table and its own measurement step (a paid pilot).

## Part 1. One real example, worked end to end

I solved one concrete case first, then kept only the ideas that behaved like it.

### Worked example: a Finnish packaging and cleaning supplies wholesaler on Netvisor with Rackbeat

**Why this business and not an HVAC or electrical wholesaler.** Finnish HVAC and electrical trade runs on shared national 7 digit product codes (LVI-numero and Sähkönumero), which wholesalers use "in product pages, orders, inventories, EDI messages and pricing" ([Sähkönumerot.fi](https://www.sahkonumerot.fi/fi/tietoa-ja-ohjeet/tuotetiedot), [Tuotetietostandardi on Wikipedia](https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuotetietostandardi), [LVI-Info](https://lvi-info.fi/tuotetiedot/)). There, customers often already send the right code, and big customers use EDI. So the matching work is smaller. I picked a trade with no shared code system I could find, where customers write their own words. Check this for any trade before you target it.

**The business.** 25 staff (small, by your definition), one warehouse near Tampere, sells packaging, cleaning and hygiene supplies to restaurants, cleaning firms, care homes and small factories. The owner decides.

**Where orders are keyed (the question that matters).** It runs **Netvisor Professional for accounting and "Netvisor Varasto by Rackbeat" for stock and orders.** In that setup, sales orders live in Rackbeat and invoices flow to Netvisor. Netvisor documents a "Rackbeat Order" process ([Netvisor: Rackbeat Order](https://support.netvisor.fi/en/support/solutions/articles/77000556711-rackbeat-order)); Netvisor Varasto starts from €70 a month, is only sold with Netvisor Professional, and gives real time stock balances ([Netvisor Varasto](https://netvisor.fi/tuote/varastonhallinta/), [Netvisor marketplace: Rackbeat](https://netvisor.fi/marketplace/integraatiot/rackbeat/)). Rackbeat is a Danish system owned by Visma since 2021 ([Nordic9](https://nordic9.com/news/rackbeat-was-acquired-by-visma/)), built for stock holding wholesalers ([Rackbeat wholesale page](https://rackbeat.com/engros/)), with an open API ([Rackbeat API](https://rackbeat.com/en/product_features/api/)). Per the search result from Netvisor's pricing sheet, opening Rackbeat and its integration interface are free of charge ([Netvisor pricing 2025](https://netvisor.fi/download/pricing/fi/netvisor-pakettihinnasto-1-2025.pdf)).

**What arrives.** Orders by email. Some are PDF purchase orders from the customer's own system. Some are Excel sheets. Many are typed in the email body, in Finnish ("2 ltk jätesäkki 125 l, 5 kpl käsipyyhe rulla..."). Customers use their own words and their own codes.

**What the person does today.**
1. Opens the email and the attachment.
2. Finds the customer in Rackbeat.
3. For each line, works out which product it means, types code and quantity, checks price and stock.
4. Saves the sales order and sends a confirmation from Rackbeat.

**Time per order by hand (vendor sourced).** "5 to 8 minutes to process end to end" ([Mirage Metrics](https://miragemetrics.com/blog/true-cost-manual-order-entry/)); 8 to 12 minutes in supplier interviews by Hobson & Company ([TradeCentric](https://tradecentric.com/blog/manual-order-processing-cost/)). Both sell automation, so I use the **low end: 5 minutes (base) and 3.5 minutes (low).**

**Time to review a prepared draft (no source; this is the key unknown).** I test three values: **1 minute** (most lines matched), **1.5 minutes** (my central guess), **2.5 minutes** (early months, when many lines still need a fix because the alias table is still learning).

**Cost of the person (sourced).** Finnish order processors average **€3,104 a month** ([Duunitori](https://duunitori.fi/palkat/tilauskasittelija)); sales secretaries about €2,500 ([Palkkavertailu](https://palkkavertailu.com/palkka/myyntisihteeri)). €3,104 × 12 = €37,250, plus about 20% employer costs (estimate) = €44,700, over about 1,650 hours = **about €27 an hour**, or €0.45 a minute.

**The formula.** Monthly saving = orders a day × minutes saved per order × 20.8 working days × €0.45. So **each order a day is worth €9.36 a month for every minute saved.**

**Sensitivity: minutes saved and value per order a day**

| Hand time | Review time | Minutes saved | Value of one order a day, per month | Orders a day needed so saving is twice a €350 fee |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 5 | 1 | 4 | €37.44 | 19 |
| 5 | 1.5 | 3.5 (base) | €32.76 | 22 |
| 5 | 2.5 | 2.5 | €23.40 | 30 |
| 3.5 | 1 | 2.5 | €23.40 | 30 |
| 3.5 | 1.5 | 2 (low) | €18.72 | 38 |
| 3.5 | 2.5 | 1 (stress) | €9.36 | **75** |

**Monthly saving at three volumes**

| Orders a day | Base (3.5 min saved) | Low (2 min saved) | Stress (1 min saved) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 20 | €655 | €374 | €187 |
| 40 | €1,310 | €749 | €374 |
| 80 | €2,621 | €1,498 | €749 |

**What we would charge.** €2,000 build (about $2,320) plus €350 a month (about $406).
* At 40 orders a day, base: the client keeps €960 a month, so the build pays back in about 2 months. Low: keeps €399, about 5 months. **Stress: keeps €24. Not worth it.**
* So 40 orders a day is **not** safe on its own. It is safe only if review time ends up at 1.5 minutes or less, or hand time is nearer 5 minutes.

**How to handle the unknown review time (the fix).**
1. **Week one:** time the clerk on 20 real orders (hand time) and count orders a day for a week.
2. **Pre qualify:** walk away if orders a day × (hand time minus 1.5) × 9.36 is below €700. At 3.5 minutes by hand, that means below 38 orders a day.
3. **Paid pilot:** the build fee is paid half at start and half at go live. The first 4 weeks live are included in the build fee. During them the system logs how long each review takes.
4. **Go or stop after the pilot:** the monthly fee starts only if measured saving is at least twice the fee. If it is lower, cut the fee to half the measured saving (never below your running cost) or hand over and stop upkeep. Say this in the proposal. It removes the owner's biggest fear and protects you from a client who will churn in month three.

**The UK version of the same case, and why it lost.** A 30 person UK wholesaler on Sage 200 with 50 orders a day. UK order processors average £26,832 a year ([Indeed UK](https://uk.indeed.com/career/order-processor/salaries)), about £19 an hour with employer costs (estimate). The saving is roughly £300 (stress) to £1,160 (base) a month. But building on Sage 200 needs the Sage 200 Developer Programme ([Sage KB](https://gb-kb.sage.com/portal/app/portlets/results/viewsolution.jsp?solutionid=200427112454709)) at **£416 a month or £4,995 a year** in July 2025 ([Sage pricing sheet](https://downloads.sage.co.uk/download/?did=cd53b8a9-506f-4d67-8ef7-4a016d7a8197)), with a rise from August 2025 ([Sage notice](https://ie-kb.sage.com/portal/app/portlets/results/view2.jsp?k2dockey=250725123504990)). With one client you pay Sage more than the client pays you. And Zestcode already sells Sage 200 order automation as a service ([Zestcode](https://zestcode.co.uk/services/sage-200-order-automation/)). So Sage 200 waits.

**What this example taught me (the rules I then used on every other idea):**
1. **Target the system where orders are typed, not the accounting system.** Busy wholesalers with stock usually key orders in an ERP or stock system and send invoices to accounting. Ask first.
2. **The saving is time, not a head.** Nobody gets fired over 3 hours a day. The owner buys for same day dispatch, fewer wrong items and no backlog when the order person is off sick. Sell those, with the hours as proof.
3. **Review time decides the sale more than volume does.** Measure it in a pilot before the monthly fee starts.
4. **The hard part is item matching, not reading the PDF.** Any AI model can read the PDF. The value is mapping "käsipyyhe rulla" to product 20431 for this customer and learning from each correction. That value is small in trades with shared national codes (HVAC, electrical), so avoid those.
5. **The ERP decides if the job is buildable and sellable.** It needs an API that creates sales orders, a free or cheap developer route, and no built in agent or ready app.
6. **Local service firms are competitors too,** not just software. In Finland they speak the buyer's language. You compete on fixed price and speed.
7. **Structured e-invoicing kills supplier invoice ideas but not order ideas.** Mandates cover invoices, not customer purchase orders.

Every workflow below is checked against these seven lessons and your seven rules.

## Part 2. India

**Honest framing.** India is your case study market. At Indian wages the labour saving is small, and funded Indian tools already exist for the main workflow. Treat paid Indian work as a bonus.

### Workflow I1 (case study build): WhatsApp orders to TallyPrime sales orders

**a. Who has it.** Wholesalers and distributors that sell to retailers: hosiery and garments, auto parts, bicycle parts, hardware, FMCG sub distributors, pharma stockists. All are common in Ludhiana. Fits **both** sizes on paper: small (owner decides) and medium (sales or accounts head owns it, often with 2 to 4 billing clerks). **In practice, only high volume small firms and medium firms can pay** (math in e and g).

**b. Steps today.**
1. Retailers or the firm's own salesmen send orders on WhatsApp: typed lists, photos of a written list, and voice notes, in Hindi, Punjabi or English.
2. A billing clerk reads or listens to each one, finds the party ledger in Tally, and types each item and quantity into a sales order or sales invoice. Stock is checked.
3. The clerk replies on WhatsApp with a confirmation or "item not in stock".
4. Data ends up in TallyPrime (sales order, then invoice and e-way bill).

**c. Software.** The clerk works in **two business tools: WhatsApp Business and TallyPrime** (sometimes Busy or Marg). Our parts are the review screen and a small connector on the Tally PC; these are our build, not client tools.

**d. Frequency and time.** Estimate for a 15 to 25 person distributor: 50 to 150 orders a day, 4 to 6 lines each, about 4 minutes each by hand. Peaks are higher in season (Diwali, and October to January for hosiery). **Check in one day** by sitting with the clerk and counting, and note what share arrives as text, photo and voice.

**e. Who does it and what it costs (sourced), and does it pay?**
* The job is advertised. Naukri lists 96 billing job vacancies in Ludhiana ([Naukri](https://www.naukri.com/billing-jobs-in-ludhiana)), and Tally operator listings ask for "Sale & Purchase Entry" ([Naukri Tally jobs](https://www.naukri.com/tally-jobs-in-ludhiana), [SimplyHired](https://www.simplyhired.co.in/search?q=tally&l=ludhiana,+punjab)).
* Pay: Ludhiana billing executive listings show ₹10,000 to ₹20,000 a month, mostly ₹13,000 to ₹18,000 ([Apna](https://apna.co/jobs/title_billing_executive-jobs-in-ludhiana), [WorkIndia listing](https://www.workindia.in/jobs/billing_executive-sector39a-ludhiana-6349089/), [WorkIndia listing 2](https://www.workindia.in/jobs/billing_executive-miller_ganj-ludhiana-6484303/)).
* At ₹16,000 a month over about 208 hours, a clerk costs about ₹77 an hour (₹1.28 a minute). Over 26 days, **each order a day is worth about ₹33 a month for every minute saved.** If review takes 1 minute instead of 4 by hand (3 saved), each order a day is worth about ₹100 a month. If review takes 2 minutes (2 saved), it is about ₹67.

| Orders a day | Saving a month (3 min saved) | Saving a month (2 min saved) | Worth paying ₹4,000 a month? (fee at most half the saving) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 50 | ₹5,000 | ₹3,300 | **No.** |
| 70 | ₹7,000 | ₹4,700 | **No.** |
| 100 | ₹10,000 | ₹6,700 | **Yes** at 3 minutes saved; **no** at 2 |
| 150 | ₹15,000 | ₹10,000 | **Yes** |
| A second clerk is about to be hired | ₹13,000 to ₹18,000 (salary avoided) | same | **Yes** |

**The jugaad test, done with numbers.** A second clerk costs ₹13,000 to ₹18,000 a month. Automation beats that only when (1) volume is 100 or more orders a day (120 or more if review takes 2 minutes), or would otherwise need a second clerk in season, (2) wrong items cost returns and angry retailers, or (3) the owner wants same day billing and dispatch. **A firm with one clerk and 50 to 70 orders a day will not save enough to pay you.** It can still be your free case study.

**The honest verdict.** There is **no clear gap** for "WhatsApp order to Tally" in India. By your own rule, **I1 fails as an Indian product.** It passes as a **case study build**:
1. The European buyer never sees Zotok. What they see is your engine reading messy orders, matching them to the item master and learning from corrections, with real numbers. That is exactly what you sell in Europe.
2. Some distributors will not have tried these tools, or will run Busy or Marg. Busy needs a partner or custom route for third party links ([Busy FAQ](https://busy.in/faqs/third-party-services/integration/1/)).

**Your answer to "why not just buy Zotok?"**: "Try it. If it works for you, use it. We will build yours free in return for your numbers and a video." If they already use Zotok or Karvan and are happy, move on.

**Technical notes (every input type the demo relies on is now in the build and tested).**
* **Tally.** TallyPrime runs an HTTP XML server (usually port 9000), and open source code imports sales orders through it ([sales order import example](https://github.com/SatyamTechsolvo/tallyerp9import), [Node.js to TallyPrime](https://github.com/JeevanKN/Tally_Integration_with-EC2-Virtual-Window-RDP-), [TallyPrime SDK](https://github.com/labs-infinitum/tallyprime-sdk)). A small local connector on the shop PC pulls approved orders and posts XML to localhost:9000. Developer A can write it in Java in a few days.
* **Voice notes.** Added as a build step: send each voice note to Sarvam's speech to text API, which supports Punjabi (pa-IN), Hindi and English and handles Punjabi English code mixing ([Sarvam Punjabi STT](https://www.sarvam.ai/apis/speech-to-text/punjabi), [Sarvam API docs](https://docs.sarvam.ai/api-reference-docs/speech-to-text/transcribe)). It costs ₹30 an hour of audio, billed by the second, with ₹100 free credit ([Sarvam pricing, via Smallest.ai](https://smallest.ai/blog/sarvam-ai-speech-to-text-pricing), [Sarvam API pricing](https://www.sarvam.ai/api-pricing)). A 30 second note costs about ₹0.25. The text then goes through the same extraction and matching as typed orders. About 8 hours of work.
* **Photos of handwritten lists.** **Unproven.** I found no test showing reliable reading of handwritten Gurmukhi item lists. So in week 2, collect 30 real photos from the client and run them through two vision models. Score lines read correctly. If fewer than about 8 in 10 lines are right, photos go to the clerk as "needs manual entry" and the demo leads with typed text and voice notes. **The pitch never depends on handwriting working.** The case study rests on the numbers (orders a day, share of lines matched without edits, minutes per order), which hold whatever the input type.
* **WhatsApp route.** Keeping the client's existing WhatsApp Business app number ("coexistence") goes through Meta's Embedded Signup, and Meta says you must already be a Solution Partner or Tech Provider to use it ([Meta Embedded Signup overview](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/embedded-signup/overview/), [onboarding app users](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/whatsapp/embedded-signup/onboarding-business-app-users/)). The app must be opened at least once every 13 days ([360dialog](https://docs.360dialog.com/partner/onboarding/whatsapp-coexistence)). So for the first client, **use a provider** such as AiSensy (from ₹1,500 a month, about $17, [AiSensy pricing](https://aisensy.com/pricing)); confirm it supports coexistence before signing. Apply to Meta's Tech Provider programme in parallel (budget 4 hours in weeks 1 to 2) so later clients need no provider. The $17 fits your $50 budget alongside a small server; pass it to the client once paid.
* **Message costs are near zero.** The retailer messages first, so your reply is inside the free 24 hour service window. Only messages you start (templates) are charged.

**g. Realistic price (INR), checked against the saving.**
* Small (5 to 49 staff): **₹15,000 to ₹25,000 setup, ₹2,500 to ₹4,000 a month**, and **only** at 100 or more orders a day (120 or more if review time is 2 minutes), or when a second clerk is about to be hired. At 100 orders a day and 3 minutes saved, the client keeps ₹6,000 to ₹7,500 after our fee; setup pays back in 2 to 4 months.
* Medium (50 to 250 staff, 2 or more clerks, 200+ orders a day): ₹50,000 to ₹1,00,000 setup, ₹8,000 to ₹12,000 a month. Saving is ₹13,000 a month or more even at 2 minutes saved. Only where you add something the tools do not (Busy or Marg, several companies in one Tally, custom scheme and discount rules).
* Your first client: free or at cost, for real numbers, a named case study and a video.

### Workflow I2 (add on only, validate first): payment screenshots to Tally receipts

**a. Who has it.** The same distributors. Retailers pay by UPI, NEFT or cheque and send a screenshot or message ("sent 25,000 for bills 1142 and 1150"). Both sizes.

**b. Steps today.** The accountant matches the bank credit to the party, creates a receipt voucher in Tally, and allocates it against the right bills. Short payments are chased on WhatsApp.

**c. Software.** WhatsApp plus the bank statement in, TallyPrime out. 3 tools.

**d. Frequency and time.** Estimate (no public source): 30 to 80 receipts a day for a mid sized distributor, 2 to 4 minutes each. Count them in one day at your case study client.

**e. Who and cost.** An accounts clerk in the I1 pay band (₹10,000 to ₹20,000 a month). At 50 receipts × 2 minutes saved × 26 days × ₹1.28, about ₹3,300 a month: only worth it bundled with I1.

**f. Existing tools and gap.** Crowded. Vyapar TaxOne (formerly Suvit) says its AI "will extract data from images, create accurate vouchers and push them directly to Tally" ([Vyapar TaxOne](https://taxone.vyapar.com/gst-reconciliation-feature)). Karvan matches payments to orders ([Karvan](https://www.karvan.ai/)). The only possible gap is bill wise allocation from the retailer's own note. **Test TaxOne and Karvan first.** If either does bill wise receipts, drop this.

**g. Price.** Add on to I1 only: ₹10,000 to ₹20,000 setup, ₹1,500 a month (estimate, kept under half the ₹3,300 saving).

* Germany: all businesses must receive structured e-invoices since 1 January 2025; issuing becomes mandatory in 2027 and 2028 ([ecosio](https://ecosio.com/en/blog/germany-e-invoicing-explained/)).
* France: receiving from 1 September 2026; SMEs issue from September 2027 ([Avalara](https://www.avalara.com/blog/en/europe/2025/09/france-e-invoicing-e-reporting-mandate-2026-2027.html)).

### Workflow E1 (top pick): emailed customer orders to draft sales orders in the client's order system (Finland first: Rackbeat, or Procountor and Netvisor for small firms; Sage 200 in the UK later)

**a. Who has it.** Stock holding wholesalers and distributors in trades **without shared national product codes**: packaging, cleaning and hygiene supplies, food and drink to restaurants and shops, industrial consumables and MRO parts, fasteners, workwear, horticulture. Also manufacturers selling spare parts to trade customers. HVAC and electrical are lower priority in Finland because of LVI-numero, Sähkönumero and EDI. **Both** sizes: small (owner or sales manager decides) and medium (customer service or operations manager owns it). Rackbeat, Procountor and Netvisor users lean small; Sage 200 users lean medium.

**b. Steps today.** An order arrives by email as PDF, Excel or typed text. An order processor finds the customer, maps each line to an item code, checks price and stock, keys the order into the order system, and sends a confirmation. UK ads describe exactly this: "check, record, track, and validate customer orders in the system (ERP SAGE)" ([Totaljobs](https://www.totaljobs.com/jobs/sales-order-processor)). **c. Software.** In: Outlook or Gmail. Out: the order system (Rackbeat, Procountor or Netvisor; later Sage 200). Middle: our review screen. 2 client tools.

**d. Frequency and time.** Not published for any market. Expect 20 to 150 orders a day for firms worth selling to. 3.5 to 5 minutes by hand (sourced); review 1 to 2.5 minutes (estimate, measured in the pilot). Pre qualify with the formula in Part 1: orders a day × (hand minutes minus 1.5) × €9.36 must reach twice the fee. At 3.5 minutes by hand and a €350 fee, that is **38 or more orders a day**; at 5 minutes, 22 or more.

**Build outline (about 100 to 120 hours).**
1. Mail hook: Microsoft Graph subscription (or Gmail push) on the orders inbox.
2. Spring Boot service (Developer A): pull the attachment, call an LLM with vision to extract customer, lines and dates as JSON.
3. Matching: a PostgreSQL table of the item master plus a per customer alias table ("käsipyyhe rulla" means item 20431 for customer X). Every correction saves a new alias.
4. Review screen (Developer B, TypeScript): email on the left, draft order on the right, low confidence fields in yellow, one Approve button. Finnish and English labels.
5. On approve: create the sales order through a thin ERP adapter (Tally, Rackbeat, Procountor, Netvisor, later Sage 200); send the confirmation.
6. **Timing log:** orders in, lines matched without edits, corrections, and **seconds from opening a draft to approving it.** This log decides the go or stop after the pilot, and it is your case study.

### Workflow E2 (validate before building): remittance advice to cash allocation

**a. Who has it.** Suppliers to big retailers and national accounts: food and drink producers, consumer goods wholesalers, building product suppliers. Mostly **medium**; small only if they sell to big chains.

**b. Steps today.** A big customer pays one lump sum and emails a long remittance (PDF or spreadsheet) listing many invoices, with short payments and deductions. A credit controller matches each line to open invoices, posts the allocation, and raises queries.

**c. Software.** In: email. Out: the ERP or accounting system. Bank lines come from the ERP's own bank feed, so **2 tools** (3 if only a bank CSV). Retailer portal remittances are out of scope (usually no public API). We never move money. **Check first** that the ERP API can post the allocation; if not, prepare a batch for the clerk to import.

**d. Frequency and time.** A vendor example: a food wholesaler supplying a national grocer "may receive a payment once a fortnight with a 12 page remittance covering 180 odd invoices" ([RemitClear](https://remitclear.com/solutions/remittance-automation-software)). ### Workflow E3 (add on only): supplier price lists into ERP and webshop
