ATTACK 1 [FATAL] The top European pick targets the accounting system, not the system where wholesalers actually key orders
Where: "Build E1 for Procountor and Netvisor in Finland as your European product." Worked example: a 25 staff HVAC wholesaler that "Runs Procountor for accounting, sales orders and invoicing" and where the processor "checks price and stock" in Procountor. Qualifying rule: "look for 40 or more orders a day".
Problem: Procountor and Netvisor are financial management systems. Finnish wholesalers that handle 40+ orders a day with a warehouse usually run order entry in an ERP such as Lemonsoft (over 6,000 customer firms, sells a wholesale order and delivery package: https://lemonsoft.fi/tukkukauppa-toiminnanohjaus/ , https://www.itewiki.fi/tarjonta/sujuvaa-tiedonkulkua-tukku-ja-erikoistavarakauppaan-lemonsoft-toiminnanohjausjarjestelmalla). Lemonsoft is even listed in Procountor's own app store as a system that pushes sales invoices into Procountor (https://procountor.fi/store/tuotteet/lemonsoft/). That means orders are keyed in the ERP and only the invoices land in Procountor. Stock in Procountor is a separate paid store add on, "Procountor Varastonhallinta", aimed at firms needing "a simple tool", and the stock balance only moves when a sales invoice is set to Approved (https://procountor.fi/store/tuotteet/procountor-varastonhallinta/). So open sales orders do not reserve stock, and the "checks price and stock" step in the worked example does not work the way the solution says for a busy wholesaler. The solution gives no evidence that even one Finnish firm at 40+ orders a day enters sales orders in Procountor or Netvisor. It reads "I found no order tool built for either of them" as a gap. The likelier reading is that there is little demand there. The filter the solution sets itself (40+ orders a day) picks exactly the firms least likely to key orders in Procountor or Netvisor. Top pick 1, the E1 build outline and weeks 11 to 16 of the plan all rest on this unchecked premise.

ATTACK 2 [MAJOR] The "no order tool found in Finland" claim misses Finnish firms that already sell this exact service
Where: "I searched in Finnish and English and found no tool built for them" and the E1 f competitor table, which lists only Zimply as a Nordic risk.
Problem: A Finnish search for "tilaustenkäsittely tekoäly" finds Finnish firms already selling AI agents that turn email orders into ERP orders. Netum: "Tilaustenkäsittely automaattiseksi tekoälyratkaisulla". Incoming orders are read and "siirretään automaattisesti käytössä olevaan järjestelmään", meaning moved into whatever system the client already uses (https://www.netum.fi/uutishuone/blogi/tilaustenkasittely-automaattiseksi-tekoalyratkaisulla/). Hurja Solutions built an agent that reads email orders as text, PDF, Excel or images and posts them to the ERP (https://hurja.fi/blogi/tekoalyn-mahdollisuudet-teollisuudelle-tehoa-tarkkuutta-ja-saastoja/). These are local, Finnish speaking, system agnostic service firms. That is the same model the user plans to sell ("set it up, tune item matching... answer the phone"). The solution's own risk list says "Finnish buyers prefer Finnish speaking support", yet it leaves out the Finnish competitors who have that edge. The competitor check the task requires ("Whether existing software already solves it") is incomplete for the one market it recommends.

ATTACK 3 [MAJOR] The worked example's core value lever (item matching) is weakest in the exact vertical it picked
Where: "Customers use their own part codes or plain words, not the wholesaler's codes" and lesson 2: "The hard part is item matching... Tools that do the matching are [a threat]." The example is a Finnish HVAC and electrical wholesaler.
Problem: Finnish HVAC and electrical trade runs on national 7 digit product codes. These are LVI-numero for HVAC, managed by LVI-Numero Oy, and Sähkönumero for electrical. Wholesalers and retailers use them "in product pages, orders, inventories, EDI messages and pricing" (https://www.sahkonumerot.fi/fi/tietoa-ja-ohjeet/tuotetiedot , https://fi.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuotetietostandardi , https://gs1.fi/fi/standardit/standardit-eri-toimialoilla/kiinteisto-ja-rakennusala/lvi-ja-sahkonumeroihin-perustuvien). In this vertical, a customer order often already carries the shared code, and the larger customers use EDI. So the "22mm kulmayhde to item 10457" matching problem, which the solution names as where the value is, is smaller here than it claims. The emailed volume that is left is also smaller. The worked example, which "every workflow below is checked against", is built on a wrong picture of how orders arrive in this trade.

ATTACK 4 [MAJOR] The go or no go threshold depends on one unsourced number, and a realistic value flips the verdict
Where: "Reviewing a prepared draft takes about 1.5 minutes (estimate). So we save 3.5 minutes an order (base) or 2 minutes (low)." Also "To be safe, look for 40 or more orders a day."
Problem: The whole sell or walk away rule rests on the 1.5 minute review time. The solution itself says item matching only improves "from each correction", so in the first months most lines need a human fix. Say review takes 2.5 minutes in the low case (3.5 minutes by hand). The saving is then 1 minute an order: 40 orders × 1 min × 20.8 days = 13.9 hours × €27 = about €374 a month. After the €350 fee the client keeps €24, the same result the solution calls "Do not sell that client" at 20 orders a day. The "safe" 40 order rule stops working, and the solution never tests how sensitive it is to this estimate. The number that should come first is review time per order during the first months. The solution has the user measure only order volume in week one ("Measure the real number in week one").

ATTACK 5 [MINOR] The price rules contradict each other
Where: Price table: "Below about 40 orders a day, a small firm cannot pay even $400 from its saving." E1 g: "Small... $400 to $600 a month... only where the measured saving is at least twice the fee (about 40+ orders a day)."
Problem: By the solution's own numbers (€33 per order a day in the base case, €19 in the low case), $400 (about €345) needs €690 of saving, which is 21 orders a day in the base case. So "below 40 cannot pay even $400" is false in its base case. At the top of the band, $600 (about €517) needs €1,034 of saving, which is 54 orders a day in the low case, not "about 40+". A user qualifying a lead at 40 orders a day and quoting $600 breaks the solution's own "fee at most half the saving" cap.

ATTACK 6 [MINOR] The Indian case study demo depends on abilities the build never includes or checks
Where: I1 b: orders arrive as "typed lists, photos of a handwritten list, sometimes voice notes, in Hindi, Punjabi or English". Also "A photo of a handwritten Punjabi order list working is a far stronger demo." Build plan weeks 3 to 8 and technical notes.
Problem: The build has no speech to text step, so voice note orders fall back to the clerk. No source or test backs reliable reading of handwritten Gurmukhi item lists, yet that is the centrepiece of the European pitch. The "no provider" WhatsApp route also needs Embedded Signup for coexistence, which the solution itself says "Meta offers to its registered partners" (Tech Provider onboarding and business verification). The 16 week plan gives this no time. If any of these fails, the plan falls back to AiSensy or to a demo on typed text. That weakens the one case study that Part 4 and top pick 2 depend on.
