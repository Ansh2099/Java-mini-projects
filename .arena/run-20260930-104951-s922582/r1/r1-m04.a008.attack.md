ATTACK 1 [FATAL] The number one pick is already a built in feature of its own first target ERP
Where: E1 "ERP | Microsoft Dynamics 365 Business Central | ... Best first target." and "The gap: ... Nobody cheap does 'match this customer's lines to my items and prices in my Business Central, then let my processor approve'. That is your product." Also Final top picks 1: "Start with Business Central because its API is the cleanest".
Problem: This is false. Business Central ships a Sales Order Agent. Microsoft's own docs say it watches a shared Microsoft 365 mailbox, reads the email body and PDF or image attachments (up to 50 pages), finds the customer, checks item availability, creates a sales quote, converts it to a sales order, and puts every step through a human reviewer (Sales Order Agent overview and FAQ, learn.microsoft.com/dynamics365/business-central/sales-order-agent and /faqs-sales-order-taker-agent). Microsoft's billing page prices it at about 1,650 Copilot Credits for 100 order emails a month (learn.microsoft.com/dynamics365/business-central/dev-itpro/administration/tenant-admin-center-manage-consumption-billing), and the pay as you go meter is $0.01 per credit (learn.microsoft.com/power-platform/admin/pay-as-you-go-meters). So 100 orders cost about $16.50 a month. The task says: "If it is already solved by a built in feature or a cheap tool, reject it." The document never mentions this agent. It scores E1 "Not solved by a cheap tool = 2" and tells the user to go to Business Central shops first, where the prospect's own ERP already does the job for a few cents an order. A BC owner will ask "why pay you $3,000 plus $550 a month when Microsoft does this for $16.50?", and the document gives the user no answer. The gate in its own matrix ("if 'not solved by a cheap tool' scores 0, the option is out") should have removed E1 for BC, which is the ERP it builds the whole plan, the pilot connector and the partner calls ("call 3 Business Central partners") around. At the very least the pitch had to be narrowed to what the agent does not handle (non BC ERPs, customer part codes, complex lines), and it was not.

ATTACK 2 [MAJOR] The "qualify rule" lets through clients who lose money on the deal
Where: E1 d. "Qualify rule: only sell where at least half of one person's day goes on keying orders." and E1 g. "one processor at £24,000. If you remove half the keying, that frees about £12,000 of time a year. Your year one price at $3,000 plus $550 a month is about $9,600, or roughly £7,400. That works, but only above the qualify rule in (d)."
Problem: The sanity check assumes the processor spends 100% of the £24,000 salary on keying (half of 100% = £12,000). At the qualify line the rule itself sets, keying is only 50% of the day, which is £12,000 of salary. Removing half of that frees £6,000 a year. The year one price is about £7,400. So a client exactly at the qualify threshold pays £1,400 more than it saves, and the break even point is really about 62% of a full time role spent on keying (7,400 / 12,000). The user is told to "walk away from anyone under the qualify rule", but the rule as written sends them to prospects where the owner is right to say no. The document's own sentence "Below it, the owner is right to say no" is wrong about where "below" starts.

ATTACK 3 [MAJOR] Sage 200 is recommended without saying its developer access costs far more than the whole budget
Where: E1 c. "Sage 200 (UK) | Usable. ... you must join the Sage 200 API Developer Programme" and Final top picks 1: "(then Exact, then Sage 200)".
Problem: The task sets a hard limit: "Our budget is under 50 USD a month until we have revenue." Sage's own July 2025 pricing (Sage 50 / Sage 200 UKI Developer Program UK Pricing, downloads.sage.co.uk, found via search; with a price increase from 1 August 2025 announced on ie-kb.sage.com) puts the Sage 200 programme at about £416 a month or £4,995 a year excluding VAT. That is more than ten times the budget. The document mentions the programme but not its price, so the user would find out only after a Sage 200 prospect says yes. It also says "these clients have budget" about Sage 200, which is about the client's budget, not the builder's.

ATTACK 4 [MAJOR] The Indian case study pick has no Indian evidence and never passes the India "beats hiring" test
Where: I2 d. "My estimate: 5 to 40 POs a day, 5 to 20 minutes each" with "vendor figures, not India specific"; I2 f. "Honest caveat: I found no hard Indian data on how common this is."; I2 g. "₹25,000 to ₹50,000 setup, ₹3,000 to ₹6,000 a month" (no source).
Problem: The task requires for every workflow "Evidence that businesses really have this problem and already spend money on it today (staff salaries, virtual assistants, outsourcing, paid tools, job postings listing the task)". I2's only India sources are generic Ludhiana billing job pages; none is shown to list "entering buyer POs into Tally" as a duty, and the document admits it has no data. The task also says an Indian workflow "only makes sense where automation clearly beats simply hiring another person". I2 has no qualify rule at all. At the low end of its own estimate (5 POs a day at 5 minutes) that is 25 minutes a day, which an existing billing clerk absorbs for free, so the automation does not beat hiring. The INR price has no source, while the task says "Say what Indian businesses would realistically pay". This is the workflow the whole plan rests on for proof of work ("Build it first for one Indian supplier"), so an unproven I2 undermines the case study bridge and top pick 2.

ATTACK 5 [MAJOR] The pain evidence for E1 does not match the ERPs it tells the user to target
Where: E1 b. "UK job ads describe exactly this: processing orders ... into Sage" (Jobsite, Totaljobs, DWP) versus E1 c. "Sage 50 | Avoid." and Final pick "Start with Business Central".
Problem: The only proof that firms pay people to key emailed orders is job ads that say "Sage", with no sign they mean Sage 200 rather than Sage 50. The document then tells the user to avoid Sage 50 and target Business Central first, for which it cites no job ad, no firm, and no count at all. QuickBooks is not mentioned anywhere, and for Xero (which has no native sales orders) it only says "check where their orders actually live" without naming a single stock tool (such as Unleashed, Cin7 or Brightpearl) or its API. The task asks to "Name the software these businesses actually use". For the small (5 to 49) buyer, which E1 claims to fit ("Both sizes"), the user is left guessing which ERP those buyers run and whether the target is reachable. Combined with Attack 1, the one ERP it does commit to is the one where the pain is already solved.

Where: Assumptions 1 "Most evidence below is from the UK, with some from the Netherlands and Belgium (Exact Online)"; Final pick 1 "(then Exact, ...)".

There is no Dutch or Belgian evidence of the pain, no salary for an order processor there, no local price, and no competitor check in that market. The user cannot price or qualify an Exact Online prospect from this document.

ATTACK 7 [MINOR] E2's scores and fallback role rest on no data
Where: Matrix row "E2 ... Not solved by a cheap tool 1 ... Total 7"; E2 d. "My estimate: 20 to 100 confirmations a day ... Measure it."; E2 f. "None publish SMB prices that I found"; closing line "drop E1 and move to E2 for medium firms."
Problem: The document found five vendors that already do E2 (Netfira, Doxis, DocBits, Hyperfox, Leverage) and no price for any of them, so it has no basis to score "not solved by a cheap tool" above 0 or to say the gap exists at all. Volume and time are unsourced estimates. Yet E2 is both the "second sale" and the fallback if E1 fails, even though the document itself says "The gap is smaller than E1". If the UK calls kill E1, the user is sent to an option that is less proven, not more.
